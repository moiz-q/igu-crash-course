Before you begin the English I lessons, take this quick diagnostic check. Do **not** use a dictionary or translator—your honest score tells you which units need extra practice.

## Part A: Grammar check (10 items)

_ While I ___ (walk) to the lab yesterday, I met my C instructor. = was walking ~~ past continuous for background action
_ By the time the server crashed, we ___ (already / save) the database. = had already saved ~~ past perfect for earlier past action
_ Could you tell me where ___ (the library / be)? = the library is ~~ indirect question uses statement word order
_ She has lived in Istanbul ___ 2023. = since ~~ point in time takes `since`
_ We have been debugging this loop ___ two hours! = for ~~ duration takes `for`
_ You ___ (not / have to) print the report; PDF upload is enough. = don't have to | do not have to ~~ lack of necessity
_ If you ___ (press) `Ctrl+S`, the editor saves your file. = press ~~ zero conditional (present simple)
_ If I ___ (have) more time tomorrow, I will review the SQL joins. = have ~~ first conditional
_ English ___ (speak) in most international software teams. = is spoken ~~ present simple passive
_ That is the classmate ___ laptop runs Linux. = whose ~~ possessive relative pronoun

## Part B: Vocabulary & collocation check (5 items)

_ Please ___ attention to the semicolon at the end of each C statement. = pay ~~ collocation: `pay attention`
_ Remember to ___ a backup before running `DROP TABLE`. = make | take ~~ collocation: `make/take a backup`
_ The new monitor looks much ___ (bright) than the old one. = brighter ~~ comparative adjective
_ Can you ___ me a favor and check my slide contrast? = do ~~ collocation: `do someone a favor`
_ Her explanation was very ___, so everyone understood pointers. = clear ~~ clear explanation

## Part C: Short reading check

Read the 120-word passage and answer the questions below:

> When first-year programming students start university in English, they often worry about accent or perfect grammar. In reality, technical clarity matters far more than sounding like a native speaker. A programmer who writes short, direct sentences in a bug report helps the whole team solve problems faster. Similarly, reading English documentation every day for just fifteen minutes builds vocabulary naturally. Instead of memorizing long lists of rare words, focus on verbs and nouns you actually meet in lectures: *compile, query, route, format,* and *debug*. Over one semester, consistent daily reading turns English from a barrier into your strongest study tool.

? According to the passage, what matters most for first-year programming students?
- Having a native-speaker accent ~~ the passage says not to worry about accent

- Memorizing rare dictionary words ~~ the passage advises against memorizing rare word lists
- Avoiding English documentation ~~ the passage recommends 15 minutes of daily reading
! Technical clarity and consistent reading matter far more than accent.

? What study habit does the author recommend?
- Translating every lecture word by word ~~ not mentioned
* Reading English documentation for 15 minutes every day ~~ builds vocabulary naturally

! Fifteen minutes of daily English reading builds practical vocabulary.

## How to interpret your score

- **14–17 / 17**: Solid B1+/B2 foundation! Move smoothly through Units 1–5 and aim straight for `en-ready`.
- **9–13 / 17**: Good intermediate base—pay close attention to **verb tenses** (`en-02`, `en-07`, `en-08`) and **articles/modals** (`en-05`, `en-06`).
- **0–8 / 17**: Take each lesson slowly, read every 300-word passage aloud, and complete all gap-fill items twice.
