Many things that were science fiction fifty years ago — video calls, robot vacuum cleaners, talking computers — are normal today. Others, like flying cars and time travel, are still fiction. In this lesson you compare the two, and learn how to write a strong **comparison paragraph**: a paragraph that shows how two things are **similar** and how they are **different**.

## Language for comparing

| Similarities | Differences |
| --- | --- |
| **Both** A **and** B are… | A is…, **whereas / while** B is… |
| A is **similar to** B **in that**… | **Unlike** A, B is… |
| **Like** A, B … | A is…; **however**, B is… |
| A …, and B **also** … / B does **too** | **In contrast**, … / **On the other hand**, … |
| **Similarly**, … / **Likewise**, … | A is **much / far / slightly** more…**than** B |
| A is **as** … **as** B | A is **not as** … **as** B |

**Comparative adjectives:** *faster, cheaper, more realistic, less expensive, better, worse.* Make them stronger or weaker with **much / far / a lot / slightly / a bit**: *Real robots are **far less** intelligent than film robots.*

## Two ways to organise a comparison paragraph

| Block method | Point-by-point method |
| --- | --- |
| Topic sentence | Topic sentence |
| Everything about A (point 1, 2, 3) | Point 1: A vs. B |
| Everything about B (point 1, 2, 3) | Point 2: A vs. B |
| Concluding sentence | Point 3: A vs. B |
| | Concluding sentence |

For one paragraph, **point-by-point** is usually clearer, because the reader sees each difference immediately.

## Worked example (point-by-point)

> **Topic sentence:** Robots in science-fiction films and robots in real life are similar in some ways, but they are very different in intelligence and appearance.
> **Point 1 (similarity):** Both film robots and real robots do jobs that are dangerous or boring for humans, such as exploring space or building cars.
> **Point 2 (intelligence):** However, film robots can usually think, talk and feel like people, whereas real robots can only do the specific tasks they were programmed for.
> **Point 3 (appearance):** Unlike the human-shaped robots in films, most real robots look like arms, boxes or small vehicles.
> **Conclusion:** In short, real robots are far more useful than they are exciting — at least for now.

## Reading: Yesterday's future (≈290 words)

> In 1966, a famous American television series showed space travellers talking to small flat devices and asking computers questions out loud. At the time, both ideas seemed as far away as travelling faster than light. Today, almost everyone carries a smartphone, and millions of people speak to digital assistants every day.
>
> It is interesting to compare how science fiction imagined the future with how it actually arrived. Some inventions appeared much faster than writers expected. **Unlike** the giant, room-sized computers in early novels, real computers became smaller and cheaper every year. **Similarly**, video calls, which looked magical in films, are now so normal that people complain about them.
>
> Other predictions, **however**, were far too optimistic. Writers in the 1950s were sure that by the year 2000 people would live on the Moon and drive flying cars. In reality, no human has walked on the Moon since 1972, and flying cars are still experimental. Building a working machine is one challenge; making it safe, cheap and legal is a much bigger one.
>
> There is also a difference in **how** technology changed life. Science fiction often showed big, visible changes — robots in every house, cities in the sky. **In contrast**, the real revolution has been mostly invisible: data, networks and software that we cannot see but use every minute.
>
> Perhaps the most important lesson is that science fiction is **not as** good at predicting gadgets **as** it is at predicting problems. Many stories warned about surveillance, fake news and dependence on machines. **Like** the inventions, these warnings have also become reality — **whereas** the flying cars have not.

? What is the **main idea** of the text?
- Science fiction is always wrong ~~ some predictions came true
* Science fiction predicted some technology well and some badly, and was better at predicting problems ~~ covers the whole text
- Flying cars will arrive next year ~~ not stated
- Computers are getting bigger ~~ opposite
! The last paragraph gives the conclusion.

? Which prediction came true **faster** than expected?
- Living on the Moon ~~ didn't come true
- Flying cars ~~ still experimental
* Small, cheap computers and video calls ~~ paragraph 2
- Cities in the sky ~~ didn't happen
! Detail question.

? "The real revolution has been mostly invisible." What does the writer mean?
- Technology does not exist ~~ no
* The biggest changes are data, networks and software that we can't see ~~ paragraph 4
- People can become invisible ~~ no
- Robots are hidden in houses ~~ no
! Read the sentence after "In contrast".

? Which linking word in the text shows **similarity**?
- however ~~ difference
- in contrast ~~ difference
* similarly ~~ similarity
- whereas ~~ difference
! *Similarly* and *likewise* show similarity.

## Try it

Write **130–160 words** comparing **one piece of technology in a science-fiction film or book with the real version today** (robots, AI assistants, space travel, self-driving cars, video calls…). Use the **point-by-point** method with one similarity and two differences.

:::reveal Show model answer
Artificial intelligence in science-fiction films and AI in real life are similar in one way, but they differ greatly in ability and in danger. Both film AI and real AI can understand human language and answer questions, so people can talk to them almost like to a person. However, film AI usually understands the world completely and makes its own decisions, whereas real AI tools only predict useful answers from the data they were trained on, and they still make simple mistakes. Unlike the evil computers in many films, real AI does not want to control humans; it has no wishes at all. In contrast, the real risks are less dramatic: false information, privacy problems and people trusting the answers too much. In short, real AI is far more limited than film AI, but its problems are just as important to discuss.
:::

:::rubric Comparison paragraph self-check
- Topic sentence names both things and says they are similar in some ways and different in others
- One similarity and at least two differences, point by point
- At least five comparison expressions (*both, whereas, unlike, in contrast, far more…than*)
- Correct comparatives (*more realistic*, not *more realisticer*)
- Concluding sentence; 130–160 words
:::

## Common mistakes

- **"more cheaper", "more better"** → *cheaper*, *better* (don't double the comparative).
- **"Unlike film robots, real robots are not like humans"** is fine, but don't forget the comma after the *Unlike…* phrase.
- **"Whereas"** at the start of a sentence with only one clause: *Whereas real robots are simple.* ✗ — it needs two clauses.
- Writing only about A and forgetting B — every point should mention both.

## Key terms

- comparison | showing how two things are similar | karşılaştırma
- contrast | showing how two things are different | zıtlık, karşıtlık
- whereas | a linking word that shows a difference between two clauses | oysa, -iken
- point-by-point | organising a comparison by discussing A and B together for each point | madde madde (karşılaştırma)
- prediction | a statement about what will happen in the future | tahmin, öngörü

## Quick check

? "Real robots are ___ intelligent than film robots."
- much less more ~~ two comparatives together
* far less ~~ intensifier + comparative
- less far ~~ wrong word order
- more lesser ~~ wrong
! *much / far / a lot* + comparative.

? Which sentence shows a **similarity**?
- Unlike films, real life is slow. ~~ difference
- Films are exciting, whereas reality is not. ~~ difference
* Both films and novels imagine the future. ~~ similarity
- In contrast, real computers are small. ~~ difference
! *Both … and* shows similarity.

? In a **point-by-point** paragraph you:
- write everything about A, then everything about B ~~ that's the block method
* compare A and B together on each point ~~ correct
- write only about differences ~~ similarities can be included
- write only about A ~~ no comparison
! Point-by-point = one point at a time, both items.
