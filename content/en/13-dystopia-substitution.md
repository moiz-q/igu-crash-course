Good writers don't repeat the same words again and again. Compare:

> ✗ *I read a dystopian novel last month. **The dystopian novel** was frightening. My friend also wants to read **a dystopian novel**.*
> ✓ *I read a dystopian novel last month. **It** was frightening. My friend also wants to read **one**.*

English has several tools for **substitution** (replacing words) and **ellipsis** (leaving words out).

## 1. Pronouns: it, they, this, that, these

- **it / they** replace a noun already mentioned: *The city had no cars. **It** was silent.*
- **this / that** can replace a whole idea: *The government controlled all the news. **This** meant nobody knew the truth.*

## 2. one / ones — replacing a countable noun

| Use | Example |
| --- | --- |
| **one** = a singular countable noun | *I need a new phone. My old **one** is broken.* |
| **ones** = a plural countable noun | *I prefer short stories to long **ones**.* |
| **the one(s) + which/that/who / with** | *Which book? — **The one** with the red cover.* |

Not with uncountable nouns: *I like hot coffee, not cold **one*** ✗ → *not cold coffee / not cold* ✓.

## 3. so / not — replacing a clause

After *think, hope, expect, believe, suppose, be afraid, guess*:

| Positive | Negative |
| --- | --- |
| *"Will robots do our jobs?" "I think **so**."* | *"I don't think **so**."* / *"I hope **not**."* |
| *"Is the exam today?" "I'm afraid **so**."* | *"I'm afraid **not**."* |

## 4. do / do so / did — replacing a verb phrase

- *She said she would finish the project, and she **did**.* (= finished the project)
- *The rules told citizens to report their neighbours, but few **did so**.* (formal)
- *"I love sci-fi." "So **do** I." / "Neither **do** I."* (for negatives)

## 5. the same / such

- *I'll have a tea. — I'll have **the same**.*
- *A world without books? I can't imagine **such** a place.*

## Utopia vs. dystopia

A **utopia** is an imaginary perfect society. A **dystopia** is an imaginary society where life is frightening and people are controlled — often by a government, a company or technology. Dystopian stories usually *warn* readers about problems in today's world.

## Reading: Why do we love dark futures? (≈290 words)

> Look at the best-selling young-adult novels and series of the last twenty years and you will find many dystopias. In **them**, teenagers fight governments that watch every move, control every meal and choose every job. Why are such stories so popular?
>
> One reason is that they take real fears and make them bigger. Today we worry about surveillance cameras, social media algorithms and climate change. A dystopian writer imagines a world where these problems have grown out of control. Reading about **one** helps us think about the dangers in our own, without feeling directly threatened.
>
> A second reason is the hero. Most dystopian stories follow a young person who questions the rules. At first, the main character obeys like everyone else. Then something happens — a friend disappears, or a secret is discovered — and she decides to resist. Readers admire characters who **do so**, because standing up to power is difficult in real life too.
>
> Utopias, the opposite kind of story, are much less common today. Writers in the past often described perfect islands or future cities without crime or poverty. Modern readers, however, tend to find **such** places a little boring — or suspicious. "If everything is perfect," they ask, "who decided what perfect means?" In fact, many dystopias start as utopias: a society that promises happiness, but only if citizens give up their freedom.
>
> Will dystopian fiction stay popular? Most publishers think **so**. As long as people feel worried about the future, they will want stories that explore it — and, perhaps, stories that show **it** can still be changed.

## Try it

Rewrite this paragraph using substitution so that **no noun phrase is repeated**.

> *In the film, the city looks like a perfect city. The citizens of the city are happy, and the citizens always smile. The government gives every citizen a new phone each year. The phones record everything. The main character starts to wonder if the government is lying. The main character thinks the government is lying. Her friend doesn't think the government is lying.*

:::reveal Show model answer
In the film, the city looks like a perfect **one**. **Its** citizens are happy, and **they** always smile. The government gives every citizen a new phone each year, and **these** record everything. The main character starts to wonder if the government is lying. She thinks **so**, but her friend doesn't.
:::

:::rubric Substitution self-check
- Uses *one / ones* for countable nouns
- Uses *it / they / its / these* clearly (the reader knows what each refers to)
- Uses *so / not* after *think / hope*
- Uses *do / does / did* to avoid repeating a verb phrase
- The new paragraph is shorter and easier to read
:::

## Common mistakes

- **"I don't think it."** → *I don't think **so**.*
- **"I hope no."** → *I hope **not**.*
- Using **one** for uncountables: *I want water, a cold one* → *I want cold water*.
- Unclear pronouns: if *it* could mean two things, repeat the noun.

## Key terms

- substitution | replacing a word or phrase with a shorter one to avoid repetition | yerine koyma (ikame)
- ellipsis | leaving out words that the reader can understand | eksiltme
- utopia | an imaginary perfect society | ütopya
- dystopia | an imaginary society where life is controlled and frightening | distopya
- surveillance | watching people closely, often secretly | gözetim

## Quick check

? "I like your bag. Where did you buy ___?"
* it ~~ the same specific bag
- one ~~ *one* = a different bag of the same kind
- so ~~ *so* replaces clauses
- ones ~~ plural
! *it* = that exact thing; *one* = another of the same type.

? "Is the café open on Sunday?" "I don't think ___."
- it ~~ wrong
- not ~~ *I don't think not* is a double negative
* so ~~ *I don't think so*
- one ~~ wrong
! *think so / don't think so*.

? Which sentence is correct?
- I prefer red apples to green one. ~~ plural needs *ones*
* I prefer red apples to green ones. ~~ plural countable → *ones*
- I prefer red apples to green it. ~~ wrong
- I prefer red apples to green so. ~~ wrong
! *ones* replaces a plural countable noun.
