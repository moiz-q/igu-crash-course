## Section 1: Grammar & Vocabulary (35 pts)

Complete each sentence with the best word or verb form:

1. While I ___ (walk) to the computer lab, I saw my roommate.
2. When we arrived at the classroom, the quiz ___ (already / start).
3. By the time the exam started, we ___ (wait) outside for forty minutes.
4. Write a polite reply that **partly agrees** with: *"Students should study only at night."*
5. She waited for ___ hour outside the library before it opened at ___ university campus.
6. Your idea sounds ___ (great), and it sounds ___ a real solution!
7. You ___ (mustn't / don't have to) print the code; PDF upload is enough.
8. Students ___ (mustn't / don't have to) share exam answers with anyone.

:::reveal Show Section 1 answers
1. `was walking` (Past Continuous background interrupted by `saw`)
2. `had already started` (Past Perfect — happened before `arrived`)
3. `had been waiting` (Past Perfect Continuous — duration up to an earlier past moment)
4. e.g. *"I agree up to a point — it's quiet at night — but we also need sleep before morning classes."*
5. `an` hour (silent `h`), `a` university (`/juː/` consonant sound)
6. `great` (`sound + adj`), `like` (`sound like + noun`)
7. `don't have to` (optional / not required)
8. `mustn't` (strictly forbidden)
:::

## Section 2: Reading Comprehension (30 pts)

Read this short passage (~180 words) and answer the questions below:

> When first-year students start writing technical reports in English, they often try to impress instructors with long, complicated sentences. In practice, clear engineering writing works best when sentences stay short and direct. Before submitting any document, writers should check three things: structure, verb tense, and tone.
>
> First, organize every paragraph around one main idea, starting with a clear topic sentence. Next, keep tenses consistent—for example, use Past Simple when describing what you tested yesterday in the lab, and Present Simple when explaining how a system always works. Finally, avoid casual slang in university emails and reports. Following these three simple habits makes your ideas easy to follow, even when explaining complex code or network diagrams.

1. What mistake do many first-year students make in technical reports?
2. Which tense should you use when describing a test you ran yesterday vs. how a system always works?
3. What three things should writers check before submitting?

:::reveal Show Section 2 model answers
1. They try to use overly long, complicated sentences instead of short, direct ones.
2. Use **Past Simple** for yesterday's test and **Present Simple** for how a system always works.
3. Structure, verb tense, and tone.
:::

## Section 3: Guided Writing (35 pts)

Write a **100–120 word** how-to paragraph or short story about **how to prepare for a university computer lab exam**. Include **narrative tenses** OR **sequence linkers + modals** (`First, Next, must, don't have to, should`).

:::reveal Show Section 3 model answer
Preparing for a practical computer lab exam is easy if you follow a clear routine. First, you should review your past lab exercises two days before the test so you don't panic at the last minute. Next, practice writing small programs or SQL queries without looking at the answers. During the exam, you mustn't use unauthorized websites or copy code from neighbors. However, you don't have to memorize every rare command if you understand the core logic. Finally, save your work every ten minutes so a sudden crash never costs you points.
:::

:::rubric Midterm writing checklist
- Clear topic sentence and logical order (100–120 words)
- Uses sequence linkers (`First, Next, Finally`) and modals (`should, mustn't, don't have to`) accurately
- Minimal grammar/spelling errors
:::
